Pre-Wedding Ukrainian Celebration

Вінкоплетення — the weaving of the wedding wreaths

What is the pre-wedding celebration?

Our pre-wedding Ukrainian celebration is a vinkoplentinna (вінкоплетення, roughly "the wreath-weaving") — one of the oldest and most beloved Ukrainian pre-wedding traditions, held in the days before the ceremony. Family and friends of both the bride and groom gather — traditionally for the first time as one company — to weave the couple's wedding wreaths by hand, accompanied by ritual songs, blessings from parents and elders, and a table that groans under the weight of food. In many families the evening is equal parts ceremony and party: solemn one moment, howling with laughter the next.

The wreaths are woven above all from barvinok — periwinkle — an evergreen that keeps its leaves through the harshest winter, and so has stood for centuries as the Ukrainian symbol of love that endures. A wreath is a circle, and a circle has no end: that's the whole point, woven into every stem. Herbs and flowers are added for blessings of health, sweetness and prosperity, and the finished wreaths are kept by the couple long after the wedding — some families preserve them for generations.

The songs matter as much as the stems. Wreath-weaving has its own repertoire of ritual songs, passed down within families, sung over the work the way sea shanties were sung over ropes. You will not be examined on the lyrics, but you will be encouraged to hum along with conviction. It is also, traditionally, the moment the two families formally join — which is why it means the world to us that you'll be there. Everyone present has a hand, literally, in making something we will carry into our marriage.

Kazimir's family have kept this tradition across generations and continents, and on 27 May it lands in Hertfordshire. No experience required: nimble fingers optional, enthusiasm mandatory. Come ready to weave, sing along phonetically, eat very well, and learn to shout Будьмо! ("Budmo!" — "let us be!") at the appropriate moments. The appropriate moments are frequent.

The deeper history of the wreath

Wreath-weaving is older than any of the churches it now happens beside. Its roots reach back to pre-Christian Slavic tradition, where the circle — like the sun, like the seasons — stood for wholeness and eternity, and evergreen periwinkle for life that outlasts winter. When Christianity arrived, the ritual didn't disappear; it folded in, the way the best traditions do, gathering blessings and psalms alongside the older songs.

The details vary beautifully by region. In parts of western Ukraine the weaving is accompanied by ritual songs called ladkanky, sung in a call-and-response older than written records; elsewhere the emphasis falls on the parents' blessing, or on the moment the wreaths are first placed on the couple's heads. Some families weave the night before the wedding; others, like Kazimir's, in the days before, so the celebration can breathe. What never changes: many hands, one circle, no end.

Traditionally the finished wreaths are kept — tucked behind an icon, pressed in a book, sewn into a keepsake — as a quiet witness to the marriage. Ours will be made by the people in that room, which is to say: by you.

Ukraine in five minutes

For those meeting Ukraine for the first time: it is the largest country entirely within Europe, with its own language (Ukrainian, not Russian — the distinction matters), a thousand-year-old capital in Kyiv, and a culture that has survived every attempt to erase it. The embroidered shirt you may wear on Thursday, the vyshyvanka, carries regional patterns like a signature — historically you could read where someone's family came from in the stitching.

You already know more Ukrainian culture than you think: the melody of "Carol of the Bells" is Shchedryk, a Ukrainian new-year folk song. Hospitality is a near-sacred duty — guests are traditionally greeted with bread and salt, and no one has ever left a Ukrainian table hungry. Consider yourself warned about Thursday's supper.

Ukraine today — why this evening carries extra weight

We can't write this page honestly without saying it plainly: Ukraine has been at war since 2014, when Crimea was annexed and fighting began in the east, and since February 2022 it has faced a full-scale invasion. Millions of Ukrainians have been displaced; families like Kazimir's, scattered across the world for generations, have watched the places their traditions come from under fire.

Which is exactly why we're doing this. Weaving these wreaths in Hertfordshire, teaching a room full of Brits and Americans to shout Будьмо!, singing the songs anyway — this is what cultural survival looks like in practice: joyful, stubborn, and shared. Your presence at our pre-wedding celebration is, in its small way, part of that.

Saying it at a wreath-weaving in Hertfordshire is a small act of solidarity, and it will mean a great deal to the Ukrainians in the room.

A saint, a swapped cloak, and this very hill (c. 300 AD)

Around the year 300 (nobody can quite agree — it was a while ago), a Roman citizen of Verulamium named Alban sheltered a Christian priest fleeing persecution. Moved by his guest's faith, Alban converted, swapped cloaks with the priest, and handed himself over in his place. He was marched up the hill outside town and executed — becoming Britain's first saint — and the hilltop where he died is the very spot where you'll be sitting. People have worshipped there continuously ever since, which makes it the oldest site of continuous Christian worship in Britain. As wedding venues go, the reviews run long.

Roman bricks, Norman ambition (793–1077)

King Offa of Mercia (of Offa's Dyke fame) founded a monastery on the site in 793. The building you'll see, though, is substantially Norman: after 1066, Abbot Paul of Caen rebuilt it at colossal scale, and — in history's finest example of recycling — his masons robbed the bricks from the abandoned Roman city of Verulamium at the bottom of the hill. Look up at the great crossing tower during the ceremony: those flat red bricks were fired by Romans a thousand years before the Normans stacked them, and they've now stood in that tower for very nearly another thousand.

Pilgrims, the shrine, and England's longest aisle

For centuries this was one of the great pilgrimage churches of Europe. Pilgrims queued at the shrine of St Alban — its carved Purbeck marble pedestal, smashed at the Reformation, was painstakingly reassembled from more than two thousand fragments and stands there still, watched over by a rare medieval oak watching loft from which monks kept an eye on the treasures. The nave they walked up is the longest of any cathedral in England — at 85 metres, the walk down the aisle is genuinely one of the longest legally available. Megan has been practising.

Wall paintings, a duke, and why locals say 'the Abbey'

Along that nave you'll spot medieval wall paintings that survived under whitewash for centuries, and the tomb of Humphrey, Duke of Gloucester — brother of Henry V and a genuine Lancastrian celebrity. The abbey became a cathedral in 1877, but locals have never stopped calling it "the Abbey". Arrive early if you can: the building rewards gawping, and the choir you may hear warming up is one of the finest in the country.

  • Batman (1989) — Wayne Manor interiors, no less
  • Sherlock Holmes (2009) and V for Vendetta (2005)
  • The Favourite (2018) — Queen Anne's palace, Oscar included
  • Rebecca (2020) and Enola Holmes (2020)

Mostly filmed in the Jacobean House and grounds beside our Old Palace — feel free to re-enact scenes at the drinks reception, within reason.

Bishop Morton builds in brick (1485)

The Old Palace was built around 1485 by John Morton, Bishop of Ely and later Archbishop of Canterbury — the same Morton whose taxation logic gave the world "Morton's Fork". His grand brick palace originally formed a full quadrangle around a central court, and its russet Tudor brickwork, laid the year the Wars of the Roses ended, is among the finest surviving medieval brick architecture in England. When Henry VIII helped himself to church property in the Dissolution, he helped himself to Hatfield too — and used it as a royal nursery, parking all three of his children there at various points: Mary, Edward, and a red-headed girl called Elizabeth.

Elizabeth under the oak (1533–1558)

It's Elizabeth who haunts the place, in the best way. She spent much of her childhood and her politically perilous twenties at Hatfield — half princess, half prisoner — reading, translating, and outwitting people who wanted her head. In November 1558, so the story goes, she was in the park (under an oak tree, tradition insists) when riders arrived to tell her that her sister was dead and she was Queen of England. She is said to have dropped to the grass and quoted the Psalms: "This is the Lord's doing; it is marvellous in our eyes." Days later she held her very first Council of State in the banqueting hall of the Old Palace — the same hall where, on 29 May, you'll be holding a glass and (we hope) dancing with abandon.

Robert Cecil's demolition-grade confidence (1607 → your dance floor)

In 1607 the palace passed to Robert Cecil, Elizabeth's spymaster's son, who swapped houses with King James I, built the vast Jacobean Hatfield House next door, and — with the confidence of a man who owned too many palaces — demolished three of the Old Palace's four wings to reuse the bricks. The banqueting hall alone survived, which turns out to be the wing we needed. The gardens around it were first laid out by John Tradescant the Elder, the great plant-hunter, who stocked them with trees and flowers never before grown in England. Five centuries of history, one dance floor. No pressure on your moves.

      First, a cultural briefing: the Spoons

      You may hear talk of "the Spoons". A Wetherspoons is a gloriously unpretentious British pub chain: the breakfasts are enormous, the prices are from another decade, the carpet is historically significant, and you order from an app without leaving your seat. It is not fancy. That is the point. Approach with an open mind and an empty stomach.

      Getting to London

      You are closer than you think. From St Albans City station, fast Thameslink trains reach St Pancras International in about 20 minutes, running several times an hour from early morning to late at night — the same line carries on through the City (Farringdon, Blackfriars, London Bridge) and down to Gatwick and Brighton, so you often step off right where you want to be. No advance ticket needed: just tap in and out with any contactless card or phone and the daily fare caps itself automatically.

      Alternatively, St Albans Abbey station runs a little branch line to Watford, and Luton Airport is about 15 minutes up the road — handy for the budget flights on the Europe page.

      Getting around London

      Once you're in town, one contactless card does everything — Tube, bus, DLR, Overground, tram and most river boats — and the same daily cap applies across all of them. You never need a paper ticket or an Oyster card as a visitor. A few official maps worth saving to your phone before you go:

      • Tube map (PDF) — the iconic one; every line and zone
      • Bus maps — area-by-area spider maps; buses are the scenic, cheap way to see the city from the top deck
      • Cycle map — the Cycleways network and Santander Cycles ("Boris bikes") docking stations
      • Walking maps — the "Legible London" wayfinding maps; central London is far more walkable than it looks
      • All TfL maps & the TfL Go app — river services, night tube, step-free access and live journey planning in one place

      Rule of thumb: two or three stops apart, walk it — you'll see more and it's often faster than going down and up again. Anything further, the Tube or a bus. Black cabs and Uber both work late into the night.

      Choose your adventure

      For sightseeing
      • The Westminster → South Bank walk: Big Ben, the Eye, the Globe, Tate Modern, Borough Market — one unbeatable afternoon on foot
      • Tower of London — Crown Jewels, ravens, a thousand years of intrigue
      • Buckingham Palace & Changing of the Guard — free, punctual, very shiny
      For culture
      • British Museum, National Gallery, Tate Modern — world-class and entirely free
      • A West End show — same-day bargains at the TKTS booth, Leicester Square
      • Evensong at St Paul's or Westminster Abbey — the free way into both
      For kids
      • Natural History Museum — free, has dinosaurs, wins automatically
      • London Transport Museum + the front seat on the top deck of any red bus
      • South Bank street performers and the carousel
      For foodies
      • Borough Market — arrive hungry, leave sideways
      • Dishoom (the queue is worth it), Padella (same warning), a proper Sunday roast at any pub with a fire
      For date night
      • Sky Garden at golden hour — the best free view in London, book ahead
      • A river walk from St Paul's to the Tower, dinner in Shoreditch
      • Jazz at Ronnie Scott's if you're feeling fancy
      For a rainy day
      • The museums (see culture — all free, all dry)
      • Leadenhall Market + the Harry Potter filming locations crawl
      • Afternoon tea somewhere warm; you're on holiday

      🧙 Harry Potter Studio Tour — 15 minutes away

      Perfect for: anyone with a pulse. The actual sets, the actual Great Hall, butterbeer of debatable quality. Book weeks ahead — it genuinely sells out. Half a day plus gawping time.

      Perfect for: romantics and people-watchers. Punt the Backs (hire a chauffeur punt, trust us), King's College Chapel, market square lunch, watch other tourists fall in the Cam. Home by supper.

      📚 Oxford — the other one

      Perfect for: book people. The Bodleian, Christ Church (Hogwarts' dining-hall inspiration), Tolkien and Lewis's pub The Eagle & Child, covered market fudge. Equally lovely as Cambridge; will deny it.

      👑 Windsor — about an hour

      Perfect for: castle maximalists. The world's oldest occupied castle, St George's Chapel, then a stroll across the bridge to Eton for scandalously photogenic streets.

      🌊 Brighton — direct on our own train line

      Perfect for: sea air and mischief. The very same Thameslink from St Albans runs straight through (~1h50): pier, pebbles, fish and chips, the Lanes for vintage shopping, the Pavilion for architectural whiplash.

      🐑 The Cotswolds — best with a car

      Perfect for: postcard hunters. Honey-coloured villages (Bourton-on-the-Water, Bibury, Stow), cream teas, sheep with better real estate than most of us. 1h30–2h each way; make it a full day.

      🛁 Bath — the ambitious option

      Perfect for: Austen fans and spa people. Roman Baths, the Royal Crescent, buns at Sally Lunn's. ~2h30 by train via Paddington — doable, better as an overnight.

      Local intelligence

      💷 Money

      Pounds sterling (£). Cards and phones work virtually everywhere; many places are now cashless. No need for wads of cash.

      🤝 Tipping

      Far gentler than home: ~10–12.5% in restaurants (often already added as a "service charge" — check the bill), nothing at the bar, round up for taxis. Nobody is chasing you down the street.

      🚗 Driving

      We drive on the left. Roundabouts are a way of life. If that sentence alarmed you, take the train — it's better anyway.

      🔌 Practicals

      Type G three-pin plugs (bring an adapter). UK ETA required before flying — apply on the official gov.uk site only. Portions smaller, pints bigger; it balances out.

      The weather, live from St Albans

      Reading the skies…

      An honest guide to British weather in late May

      Statistically: highs around 17–20°C (63–68°F), lows around 9–11°C (48–52°F), long light evenings (sunset after 9pm), and roughly a one-in-three chance of some rain on any given day. In practice, late May can serve a heatwave, a downpour and a stiff breeze before lunch. The British solution is layers; the British attitude is cheerful denial. Pack one warm layer, one compact umbrella, sunglasses (optimism), and shoes that can survive damp grass.

      Getting here & getting around

      Which airport should I fly into?

      London Luton (LTN) is closest — about 20 minutes from St Albans. Heathrow (LHR) has the most transatlantic routes (~45–60 min away). Gatwick (LGW) and Stansted (STN) also work. If prices are similar, choose Luton or Heathrow.

      How do I get from the airport to St Albans?
      • Luton: shuttle to Luton Airport Parkway, then Thameslink direct to St Albans City (~10 min), or a 25-minute taxi.
      • Heathrow: Elizabeth line to Farringdon, change to Thameslink north (~1h15 total), or taxi (~45–60 min).
      • Gatwick: Thameslink runs direct from Gatwick to St Albans — one train, no changes (~1h15).
      • Stansted: Stansted Express to Tottenham Hale, tube to Kings Cross/St Pancras, Thameslink onward (~1h30), or taxi (~50 min).
      How do I get into London?

      Thameslink from St Albans City to London St Pancras takes about 20 minutes, with frequent trains. Tap in and out with a contactless card or phone — no ticket needed.

      Taxis and rideshare apps?

      Uber and Bolt both operate locally, though coverage thins late at night — local firms are more dependable: [PLACEHOLDER: 2–3 local taxi numbers — one St Albans, one Hatfield]. Pre-book late-night rides.

      Do I need a visa? What about plugs and cards?
      • Entry: US (and most non-European) visitors need a UK ETA — a quick online authorisation. Apply via the official gov.uk site before flying; ignore lookalike sites charging extra.
      • Plugs: UK uses the chunky three-pin Type G plug, 230V. Bring an adapter.
      • Cards: contactless everywhere, including all trains and buses. Amex is accepted less widely than Visa/Mastercard.
