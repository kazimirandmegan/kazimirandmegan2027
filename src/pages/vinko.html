What is the Vinkoplytenna?

The Vinkoplytenna is one of the oldest Ukrainian pre-wedding traditions: family and friends of both bride and groom gather — traditionally for the first time as one company — to weave the couple's wedding wreaths by hand, accompanied by ritual songs, blessings from elders, and a feast. In many families the evening is equal parts ceremony and party. The wreaths are woven from barvinok (periwinkle), an evergreen that keeps its leaves through the harshest winter and has stood for centuries as the Ukrainian symbol of enduring love. A wreath is a circle, and a circle has no end: that's the whole point, woven into every stem.

Kazimir's family have kept this tradition across generations and continents. For Megan, this evening is a moment to step into and take ownership of her part in a new culture. No experience required — come ready to weave, eat well, and learn to shout Будьмо! ("Budmo!" — the toast) at the appropriate moments. Everyone present has a hand, literally, in making something we will carry into our marriage.

The deeper history of the wreath

Wreath-weaving is older than any of the churches it now happens beside. Its roots reach back to pre-Christian Slavic tradition, where the circle — like the sun, like the seasons — stood for wholeness and eternity, and evergreen periwinkle for life that outlasts winter. When Christianity arrived, the ritual didn't disappear; it folded in, the way the best traditions do, gathering blessings and psalms alongside the older songs.

The details vary beautifully by region. In parts of western Ukraine the weaving is accompanied by ritual songs called ladkanky, sung in a call-and-response older than written records; elsewhere the emphasis falls on the parents' blessing, or on the moment the wreaths are first placed on the couple's heads. Some families weave the night before the wedding; others, like Kazimir's, in the days before, so the celebration can breathe. What never changes: many hands, one circle, no end.

Traditionally the finished wreaths are kept — tucked behind an icon, pressed in a book, sewn into a keepsake — as a quiet witness to the marriage. Ours will be made by the people in that room, which is to say: by you.

Ukraine in five minutes

For those meeting Ukraine for the first time: it is the largest country entirely within Europe, with its own language (Ukrainian, not Russian — the distinction matters), a thousand-year-old capital in Kyiv, and a culture that has survived every attempt to erase it. The embroidered shirt you may wear on Thursday, the vyshyvanka, carries regional patterns like a signature. Historically you could learn where someone's family came from in the stitching.

You already know more about Ukrainian culture than you think: the melody of "Carol of the Bells" is Shchedryk, a Ukrainian new-year folk song. Hospitality is a near-sacred duty — guests are traditionally greeted with bread and salt, and no one has ever left a Ukrainian table hungry.

Ukraine today — why this evening carries extra weight

We can't write this page honestly without saying it plainly: Ukraine has been at war since 2014, when Crimea was annexed and fighting began in the east and since February 2022 it has faced Russia's full-scale invasion. Millions of Ukrainians have been displaced and killed; families like Kazimir's, scattered across the world in diasporas for generations, have watched the places their traditions come from under fire.

Which is exactly why we're doing this. Weaving these wreaths in Hertfordshire, teaching a room full of Brits and Americans to shout Будьмо!, singing the songs anyway — this is what cultural survival looks like in practice: joyful, stubborn, and shared. Your presence at our Vinkoplytenna is, in its small way, part of that.

About that last pair. Slava Ukraini is more than a slogan. It emerged among Ukrainians fighting for independence in the early twentieth century, was suppressed for decades under Soviet rule, and returned as the greeting of a free Ukraine — spoken at the Maidan in 2014, adopted as the official salute of Ukraine's armed forces in 2018, and heard around the world since the full-scale invasion of 2022. Call, and response: one voice affirms the nation, the other honours those who defend it. Saying it at a wreath-weaving in Hertfordshire is a small act of solidarity, and it will mean a great deal to the Ukrainians in the room.
