Vinkopletyny

Вінкоплетини — the weaving of the wedding head-wreaths

Pronounced: Vin-ko-ple-TEN-nia

What is the Vinkopletyny?

Vinkopletyny is one of the oldest Ukrainian pre-wedding traditions. The divych vechir (the bride's evening) is held the night before the wedding, and its customs vary by region. Ours will be a hybrid, drawn mostly from Bukovyna, where Constance's mum, Baboo, is from. The families and friends of the bride and groom gather, traditionally for the first time as one company, to weave the couple's wedding wreaths by hand, accompanied by ritual songs, blessings from the elders, and a feast.

The evening is equal parts ceremony and party. The wreaths are woven from barvinok, periwinkle, an evergreen that keeps its leaves through the harshest winter and has stood for centuries as the Ukrainian symbol of enduring love. The wreath is a circle without beginning or end; in weaving it, the two families are themselves woven together. Kazimir's family have kept this tradition across generations and continents. For Megan, the evening is a chance to step into her new culture, to make it her own, and to bring the two families together. No experience required!

Come ready to eat well, drink, dance, sing, and learn to shout Будьмо! ('Budmo!'), for each toast.

🎉 The evening

The evening opens with a procession. Led by musicians, we parade to the church, where the couple, their parents and their godparents greet us with the коровай (korovai)*, salt, honey and a shot of Ukrainian honey vodka. Once everyone is seated, the families banter and negotiate until they reach full acceptance and the wedding can proceed as planned. The parents then give their blessings, and the evening unfolds: Ukrainian food, folk music and a bandura** player, weaving the vinok, and decorating the гільце (hiltse) 'Tree of Life'. It ends, as it should, with a great deal of dancing, so get those Kozak moves! The Tamadas are Tom Monastyrski and Iris Ciona.

What is the significance of the 'Tree of Life' (Hyltsia)?

Whilst the wreaths were being woven, the bride's elder brother (druzhba) or a close friend would bring a freshly cut wedding tree. In Bukovina, a young pine (so that life might be evergreen and abundant) or a fruit tree (cherry or apple) symbolises the 'Tree of Life' and the coming together of two families. Typically it is decorated with paper, ribbons, coins, pinecones, candles, flowers, and berries, all symbolising personal good wishes for the couple. Kazimir's family has a tradition where guests bring small gifts, photos, and ornaments to hang on the tree, each with memories of the couple. Each Christmas, Megan and Kazimir's family have gifted ornaments to them, and together with the Hyltsia decorations, will make up the ornaments for their first Christmas together.

The deeper history of the wreath

Wreath-weaving is older than any of the churches it now happens beside. Its roots reach back to pre-Christian Slavic tradition, where the circle — like the sun, like the seasons — stood for wholeness and eternity, and evergreen periwinkle for life that outlasts winter. When Christianity arrived, the ritual didn't disappear; it folded in, the way the best traditions do, gathering blessings and psalms alongside the older songs.

The details vary beautifully by region. In parts of western Ukraine the weaving is accompanied by ritual songs called ladkanky, sung in a call-and-response older than written records; elsewhere the emphasis falls on the parents' blessing, or on the moment the wreaths are first placed on the couple's heads. Some families weave the night before the wedding; others, like Kazimir's, in the days before, so the celebration can breathe. What never changes: many hands, one circle, no end.

Traditionally the finished wreaths are kept — tucked behind an icon, pressed in a book, sewn into a keepsake — as a quiet witness to the marriage. Ours will be made by the people in that room, which is to say: by you.

What is the Korovai?

A коровай (korovai) is the Ukrainian ritual wedding bread: a large, round, enriched loaf, baked from wheat flour with eggs and butter, and decorated with dough ornaments such as braids, birds (usually a pair, for the couple), ears of wheat, flowers, periwinkle leaves and sometimes a sun or moon. Its round form carries the same meaning as the wreath: wholeness, the cycle of life, the union of two families.

What is a bandura?

The bandura (ban-DU-ra) is the Ukrainian national string instrument: a lute-shaped, plucked instrument with a short neck and a broad, rounded body carrying a fan of many strings; the modern concert version has fifty or more, giving it a harp-like sound. From the seventeenth century, it was the instrument of the kobzari, itinerant, often blind minstrels who sang epic ballads and historical songs. Stalin persecuted the kobzari, and the bandura survived through diaspora communities and professional ensembles; today it remains one of Ukraine's strongest emblems of cultural memory.

A history of Ukraine

Written by a resident Ukrainian historian and soon to be Dr., Constance Uzwyshyn.

c. AD 882 — Kyiv became the centre of Kyivan Rus′, a powerful East Slavic state that controlled important trade routes between Scandinavia and Byzantium. At its height, Kyivan Rus′ was one of Europe's largest and most influential polities, with Kyiv often described as one of the continent's greatest cities.

AD 988 — Prince Volodymyr the Great adopted Byzantine Christianity and ordered the baptism of Kyivan Rus′. This moment shaped the religious, cultural and political development of Ukraine and much of Eastern Europe. Notably, Kyiv had already been a major centre of civilisation for more than a century before Moscow was first mentioned in historical records.

AD 1147 — Moscow appeared in written records for the first time as a small settlement, while Kyiv was already an established political, religious and commercial capital with international connections stretching from the Baltic to Constantinople.

AD 1240 — The Mongols sacked Kyiv after a devastating siege, bringing the era of Kyivan Rus′ to an end. Large parts of the city were destroyed, and political power shifted elsewhere, marking one of the great turning points in Eastern European history.

14th–16th centuries — Most Ukrainian lands became part of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and later the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth. While cities prospered through trade, tensions grew over serfdom, political representation and religious differences.

1648 — Bohdan Khmelnytsky led a major Kozak uprising against Polish rule. The rebellion quickly evolved into a struggle for self-government and resulted in the creation of the Kozak Hetmanate, one of the earliest expressions of a distinct Ukrainian political state.

1654 — Seeking military support against Poland, the Hetmanate concluded the Pereiaslav Agreement with Muscovy. What began as an alliance gradually enabled Moscow to extend its control over Ukrainian affairs, a development whose meaning remains fiercely debated by historians.

1775–1795 — Catherine II destroyed the Zaporozhian Sich, the traditional stronghold of the Kozaks, while the Partitions of Poland divided most Ukrainian territory between the Russian and Habsburg Empires. Ukrainian autonomy was largely eliminated, but national identity survived through language, culture and local traditions.

1917–1921 — Following the collapse of the Russian Empire, Ukrainians declared independence and fought a series of wars involving Bolshevik, White Russian, Polish and other forces. Despite determined resistance, most of Ukraine was incorporated into the Soviet Union.

1932–1933 — Millions died during the Holodomor, a catastrophic famine caused by Soviet collectivisation policies and grain requisitioning. Ukraine regards the Holodomor as a genocide, and it remains one of the defining tragedies of the nation's modern history.

1991 — With the collapse of the Soviet Union, Ukraine voted overwhelmingly for independence. More than 90% of voters supported statehood, establishing the modern sovereign Ukrainian state with Kyiv as its capital.

2014 — Following the Revolution of Dignity, Russia annexed Crimea and conflict erupted in eastern Ukraine. These events fundamentally reshaped Ukrainian politics, national identity and relations with Europe and Russia.

2022 — Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine. Expectations of a rapid victory were overturned by fierce Ukrainian resistance, and cities such as Kyiv, Kharkiv and Mykolaiv became symbols of the country's determination to remain independent.

Today — Ukraine is the largest country wholly in Europe, stretching from the Carpathian Mountains to the Black Sea. Its history spans Kyivan Rus′, Kozak statehood, imperial domination, Soviet rule and modern independence. Despite repeated invasions and attempts to extinguish its autonomy, Ukraine has preserved a distinct national identity and remains at the centre of some of the most consequential events in contemporary Europe.

Ukraine today — why this evening carries extra weight

As of late 2026, the war remains at a critical juncture, with positional fighting along the eastern and southern fronts and sustained aerial attacks on Ukrainian cities. A Ukrainian wedding in wartime is not an escape from that reality but an answer to it. Weddings have gone on in Kyiv, Lviv and Kharkiv throughout the war, often between people with days of leave, because the point of the invasion is to erase a culture, and the point of a vinok, a korovai and a hiltse is to carry it forward. Every song sung tonight, every toast of Будьмо!, is a small act of continuity. Megan joining this family, and learning its customs, is the same act in another key: the circle widening rather than closing.

About that last pair. Slava Ukraini is more than a slogan. It emerged among Ukrainians fighting for independence in the early twentieth century, was suppressed for decades under Soviet rule, and returned as the greeting of a free Ukraine — spoken at the Maidan in 2014, adopted as the official salute of Ukraine's armed forces in 2014, and heard around the world since the full-scale invasion of 2022. Call, and response: one voice affirms the nation, the other honours those who defend it. Saying it at a wreath-weaving in Hertfordshire is a small act of solidarity, and it will mean a great deal to the Ukrainians in the room.
