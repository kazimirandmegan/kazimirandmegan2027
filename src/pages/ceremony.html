A Brief History of St Albans Cathedral

c. AD 300 — A Roman citizen named Alban sheltered a Christian priest fleeing persecution. Inspired by the priest's faith, Alban converted to Christianity, exchanged clothes with him, and was arrested in his place. He was executed on the hill where the Cathedral now stands, becoming Britain's first saint. Remarkably, people have worshipped on this site ever since, making it the oldest place of continuous Christian worship in Britain.

AD 793 — According to tradition, King Offa of Mercia founded a monastery here to honour St Alban. The saint's shrine soon attracted pilgrims from across Europe and became the reason the town of St Albans grew up around the Abbey.

1077 onwards — Following the Norman Conquest, Abbot Paul of Caen rebuilt the Abbey on an extraordinary scale. In a masterclass of medieval recycling, much of the Cathedral was constructed using Roman bricks taken from the ruins of nearby Verulamium. Today, the nave remains the longest of any cathedral in England, making for a particularly memorable walk down the aisle.

1361 — A local monk, Brother Thomas Rocliffe, is said to have created the original "Alban Bun" to distribute to the poor on Good Friday. It is widely regarded as the precursor to the modern hot cross bun, giving St Albans a strong claim to one of Britain's favourite baked goods.

1539–1877 — The Abbey survived the Dissolution of the Monasteries and later became St Albans Cathedral when the Diocese of St Albans was created in 1877.

Today — The Cathedral remains at the heart of the city, which traces its roots back to the Roman town of Verulamium and has witnessed everything from medieval pilgrimage to the Wars of the Roses. Each summer, Alban Day celebrations retell the saint's story with giant puppets, proving that nearly 2,000 years on, St Alban still knows how to draw a crowd.
