A Brief History of Hatfield House

c. 1485 — The story begins with the Old Palace, built by John Morton, Bishop of Ely and later Archbishop of Canterbury. Constructed in the same year the Wars of the Roses ended, it remains one of the finest surviving examples of medieval brick architecture in England.

1538–1558 — When Henry VIII acquired Hatfield during the Dissolution of the Monasteries, it became a royal residence and nursery for all three of his children: Mary, Edward, and Elizabeth. Elizabeth spent much of her youth here, studying, reading, and navigating the political dangers of Tudor England.

1558 — Hatfield's most famous moment came when the young Elizabeth learned she had become Queen of England. Tradition says she was sitting beneath an oak tree in the park when riders arrived with the news. Days later, she held her first Council of State in the Old Palace's banqueting hall.

1607 — King James I exchanged Hatfield with Robert Cecil, chief minister and son of Elizabeth's legendary spymaster. Cecil built the magnificent Jacobean Hatfield House next door, one of the great prodigy houses of England.

1611 — With the confidence of a man who apparently had more palace than he needed, Cecil demolished three wings of the Old Palace and reused the bricks elsewhere. Fortunately, the banqueting hall survived, which is rather convenient for us.

17th Century — The gardens were laid out by John Tradescant the Elder, one of England's first great plant hunters, who filled them with exotic trees and flowers never previously grown in the country.

Today — Hatfield has remained home to the Cecil family for over 400 years and has welcomed everyone from Tudor monarchs to modern film crews. It is one of the few places in England where you can stand in the room where Elizabeth I began her reign, then step outside to wander gardens that have been astonishing visitors for centuries.
