Getting around St Albans

St Albans is a walking city; the city centre, Cathedral, Verulamium Park, and most of our recommended spots are within 15–20 minutes on foot. The main train station is St Albans City (Thameslink Train, 20 min to London). The St Albans Abbey station is a smaller branch-line terminus to the west (ignore this).

Taxis are plentiful, quick and relatively cheap. Uber and Bolt both operate locally. Buses cover the wider area but a walk or a taxi is usually easier.

St Albans is a historic cathedral city in Hertfordshire, England, located 20 miles north of London. It is famous for its rich Roman heritage (originally the ancient city of Verulamium), stunning medieval architecture and vibrant market culture. St Albans takes its name from the first British saint, Alban.

A Brief History of St Albans

c. 20 BC — The settlement that would eventually become St Albans began as the tribal capital of the Catuvellauni, one of the most powerful Iron Age peoples in southern Britain. Centred near modern-day St Michael's, it controlled trade routes across the region and laid the foundations for the city's future importance.

AD 43–50 — Following the Roman invasion of Britain, the settlement developed into Verulamium, one of the largest and wealthiest towns in Roman Britain. Granted the rare status of a municipium, its citizens enjoyed rights similar to those of towns in Italy itself. Grand villas, mosaics, theatres and temples transformed the area into a thriving provincial city.

AD 61 — The Roman city of Verulamium was burned to the ground during the revolt led by Queen Boudicca against Roman rule. Archaeologists have uncovered a distinctive layer of ash and destruction beneath the modern city, preserving one of the most dramatic moments in Britain's ancient history. The Romans quickly rebuilt the city on an even grander scale.

5th–7th centuries — After the Romans departed, Verulamium gradually declined, but settlement continued nearby. The memory of St Alban's martyrdom helped preserve the area's significance during a period when many former Roman towns were abandoned.

AD 793 — Tradition states that King Offa of Mercia founded a monastery dedicated to St Alban. The growing religious community attracted pilgrims, merchants and craftsmen, helping a new medieval town emerge beside the old Roman ruins.

1213 — At St Albans Abbey, a gathering of rebellious barons is said to have renewed demands that King John recognise ancient English rights and liberties. Historians regard this meeting as an important step on the road to Magna Carta, which was sealed just two years later in 1215 and became one of the world's most influential constitutional documents.

1381 — During the Peasants' Revolt, local residents joined the uprising against unpopular taxes and feudal restrictions. Rebels attacked property belonging to the Abbey and demanded greater freedoms, illustrating the social tensions that shaped late medieval England.

1455 & 1461 — Two major battles of the Wars of the Roses were fought in and around St Albans. The First Battle of St Albans in 1455 is often regarded as the opening battle of the conflict, while the Second Battle followed in 1461. Few English towns can claim to have hosted not one but two battles that helped determine the fate of the crown.

16th–18th centuries — Positioned on the main road between London and the North, St Albans prospered as a coaching town. Its streets became famous for inns, coaching yards and travellers, with some historic inns still standing today.

1869 — Football pioneer Ebenezer Cobb Morley, a resident of St Albans, played a leading role in establishing the Football Association and drafting the first standardised laws of the game. The city therefore has a surprising connection to the birth of modern football.

1887 — St Albans was formally granted city status in recognition of its cathedral and historic importance, cementing its place as one of England's smallest but most historic cities.

Today — St Albans is a city where nearly every era of British history remains visible. Roman walls and mosaics sit alongside medieval streets, Tudor inns and Victorian landmarks. From Iron Age tribal capital to Roman city, medieval pilgrimage centre, battlefield and modern commuter city, St Albans has repeatedly reinvented itself while preserving an extraordinarily rich historic landscape.

Choose your adventure

Must-see
  • St Albans Cathedral — Free entry. One of England's oldest and most beautiful medieval cathedrals — you'll be here on Saturday, so get a preview. Don't miss the medieval wall paintings and the tower tours.
  • Verulamium Museum & Park — Roman Britain at its most vivid. The park has a genuine mosaic and hypocaust in situ, plus a lake, a playground, and a splash park in summer. The museum is wonderful.
  • The Clock Tower — England's only surviving medieval town belfry, right in the city centre. Climb it for the view and the history; it costs almost nothing and is genuinely spectacular.
  • The Street Market (Wed & Sat) — Trading since 1553, older than the reception venue. Fresh produce, street food, local makers — one of the best in Hertfordshire.
For eating, drinking & pubs

We've been sampling restaurants around St Albans in part because we love food, but also so we could put a solid recommendations list here for you. Enjoy the fruits of our labour:

Breakfast

  • Toast — An incredible brunch spot. Americans: try bubble and squeak here — a classic British breakfast, and Mother of the Bride-approved.
  • The Waffle House St Albans — Near where many of you are staying at St Michael's. This is the polar opposite of the American Waffle House — really yummy and a beautiful sit-down place for brunch.
  • Hatch and Street Cafe — Also solid breakfast options. The Breakfast Club is great and right near our house.
  • Proto Artisan Bakery — Town's best pastries (our humble opinion).
  • Silver Palate St Albans — Very centrally located town classic (though a bit pricey).

Coffee

  • Megan has sampled all the local cafés and safely recommends: Brad & Dills, Nkora, Charlie's Coffee, and Baruccio — in addition to the coffee at all of the breakfast spots above.

Lunch & dinner

  • La Cosa Nostra — One of our favourite restaurants in St Albans. A five-minute walk from the Uzwyshyn-Jones residence, well-priced, and super authentic Italian.
  • Oasis — Our other favourite. The Moroccan food is so good and the portions/value for money is insane. Super easy as a takeaway too.
  • Per Tutti — A beautiful Italian restaurant near the Cathedral.
  • Won — Recently opened; great Korean food (though a bit pricey).
  • Sukawatee — A nice local Thai place in the city centre. Thai Square is another good option, very central and in a historic building.
  • For British food: local pubs like Hare & Hounds (also Constance's favourite pub) and the White Lion.
  • For slightly nicer options: Mediterranean food at Tabure, or elevated pub fare at Dylan's at The Kings Arms.

Drinks & sweets

  • Darlish — Potentially the best ice cream you'll ever have. Locally made and on the walk from the city centre to our house. Keep an eye out for opening times — it closes annoyingly early.
  • Mad Squirrel Tap — A local craft brewery worth trying, as is Beer Shop, which has a lot of Belgian beers.
  • No Nuisance Bar — Named after the iconic sign on the St Albans Clock Tower. A cool place on George Street, right by the Cathedral and gorgeous at night.
  • Bishop's Cave and MUST — Nice wine bars with cosy interiors.

Pubs

  • Ye Olde Fighting Cocks — Claimant to England's oldest pub; conduct your own peer review. Right next to the Cathedral.
  • Hare & Hounds — Constance's favourite pub, and easy to see why.
  • The Horn, The Jolly Sailor, The White Swan — The after-party shortlist, available for reconnaissance.
  • No Nuisance Bar — Named after the famous Clock Tower sign; great atmosphere at night.
For history & families

History

  • The Roman Mosaic and Hypocaust — Exquisitely preserved Roman mosaic in a modern building in Verulamium Park.
  • Roman Theatre of Verulamium — The only visible example of its kind in Britain, still used today.
  • Watch a play at the Roman Theatre — The Open Verulamium Organisation puts on outdoor performances in the ancient theatre itself — one of the most atmospheric ways to spend an evening in St Albans.
  • de Havilland Aircraft Museum — Britain's first aviation collection, including the unique WW2 Mosquito prototype.
  • History tours of St Albans — Guided walks and tours covering Roman, medieval and Tudor St Albans. A great way to get under the skin of the city in a couple of hours.
  • Hatfield House tours — The Jacobean house next to our reception venue is open for tours and events. Walk through the rooms where Elizabeth I began her reign and explore gardens laid out by one of England's first great plant hunters.

Families

  • Warner Bros. Studio Tour — The Making of Harry Potter — See iconic sets from The Great Hall to Platform 9¾. Book well ahead.
  • Willows Activity Farm — Engineered to exhaust the under-tens.
  • The Cathedral café + lawns — Step-free, toddler-tolerant.
  • Clarence Park — St Albans' largest park, with a bandstand, tennis courts, a bowling green, and a lovely café. Perfect for a relaxed family morning.
  • Books On The Hill — If you ask Megan what she loves about St Albans, Books On The Hill leads her list: the best bookshop she's ever found, with beautiful historical reading rooms connected. You can spend hours here.
For walkers & cyclists

Walking

  • River Ver strolls — Walk straight from the city centre along the River Ver; peaceful and surprisingly countryside-feeling for a city walk.
  • Gorhambury Estate — Countryside miles past Francis Bacon's ruined manor, with Roman, Elizabethan and 18th-century history at every turn.
  • The Alban Way — Runs along the former route of the Hatfield to St Albans railway (opened 1865, closed 1969). A lovely traffic-free route through the Hertfordshire countryside.
  • St Albans Pilgrim Trails — Self-guided trails through the city's ancient churches and historic streets.
  • Country walks in Hertfordshire — The wider district has ample routes through the beautiful Hertfordshire countryside, enjoyable all year round.

Cycling

  • Chiltern Hills cycling — The Chilterns AONB offers some of the finest cycling in the South East, with routes ranging from gentle valley rides to more challenging hill climbs through ancient beech woodland. St Albans sits on the eastern edge and is an excellent starting point.
  • The Alban Way by bike — The traffic-free Alban Way is also a great cycling route, running from St Albans to Hatfield through countryside and former railway tunnels.
  • Bike hire in St Albans — Several local hire options are available in and around the city centre. Search closer to your visit for current availability and opening times.
